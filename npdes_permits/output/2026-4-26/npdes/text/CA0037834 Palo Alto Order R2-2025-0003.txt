FACILITY DESCRIPTION
2.1. Sanitary Sewer System  and Treatment
2.1.1.  Location and Service Are a. The treatment plant is located at 2501 
Embarcadero Way  in Palo Alto. The plant provides advanced secondary 
treatment of domestic, commercial, and industrial wastewater collected from the 
cities of Los Altos, Los Altos Hills, Palo Alto, and Mountain View; the East Palo 
Alto Sanitary District; and the unincorporat ed area of the Stanford University 
Campus. The service area population is approximately 23 4,000.
Attachment B provides a map of the area around the treatment plant .
2.1.2 . Sanitary Sewer System . For purposes of this Order, the sanitary sewer  system 
is the portion of the wastewater collection system  owned and operated by t he 
City of Palo Alto . The City of Palo Alto’s  sanitary sewer  system consists of 
approximately 200 miles of pipes ranging from 4 inches to 72 inches in diameter 
and one small lift station. Satellite systems owned and operated by the East 
Palo Alto Sanitary District, Stanford University, and the cities of Mountain View, 
Los Altos, and Los Altos  convey wastewater into  the City of Pa lo Alto  sanitary 
sewer system. Each satellite collection system is separately responsible for an 
ongoing program of maintenance and capital improvements for sewer lines and 
pump stations within its respective jurisdiction to  ensure adequate capacity and 
reliability. The responsibilities include managing overflows, controlling inflow 
and infiltration, and implementing collection system maintenance . 
2.1.3.  Wastewater Treatment . The treatment process consists of preliminary 
screening, grit removal, primary sedimentation, biological treatment (fixed film 
reactors and activated sludge  basins ), secondary clarification, dual-media 
filtration, and ultraviolet (UV) disinfection. The hydraulic capacity of all treatment 
units is 80 MGD, except for the fixed film reactors and dual media filters, which 
each have a capacity of 40 MGD. The plant’s design flow is 39 MGD, 
determined by the process capacity of the fixed film reactors and the dual 
media filters. From June  2019 through February 2024 , the plant’s annua l 
average daily flow was 18.7 MGD, and the maximum daily flow was 51.7 MGD .
Attachment C provides flow schematics  for the treatment plant .
2.1.4.  Sludge and Biosolids Management . Solids from primary sedimentation tanks 
and aeration tanks are sent to the sludge thickening facilities, gravity -thickened, 
and dewatered by belt presses.  Dewatered sludge is shipped offsite for 
treatment to Class A pathogen reduction standards and  subsequently land -
applied . Belt press filtrate is returned to the plant headworks .

2.1.6.  Recycled Water Activities.  Approximately 695 acre -feet per year or 0.62 MGD 
of final effluent undergoes additional filtration and chlorination prior to 
distribution as tertiary unrestricted recycled water. The Discharger sends this 
water to the Palo Alto Golf Course, Palo Alto parks , the California Department 
of Transportation, and the City of Mountain View recycled water system. The 
water is also distributed by truck for landscape irrigation, dust control, soil 
compaction, and collection system clea ning. These reclamation activities are 
regulated under Regional Water Board Order No. 93 -160.
The Discharger ’s planned Advanced Water Purification System w ould improve 
recycled water quality by adding additional membrane filtration and reverse 
osmosis for up to 1.125 MGD of the recycled water flow , with the potential to 
expand to up to 2.25 MGD . Up to 0.2 MGD of reverse osmosis concentrate 
would be blended with advanced -secondary effluent prior to discharge .
2.2. Discharge Point and Receiving Waters
Approximately 95 percent of the treated effluent not recycled is discharged through 
Discharge Point 001 to an unnamed channel that leads to South San Francisco 
Bay. The remaining approximately 5 percent ( about  1 MGD) enters Renzel Marsh 
Pond, where it flows via a sluice gate to Matadero Creek at Discharge Point 002. 
Matadero Creek flows into Mayfield Slough within the Palo Alto Flood Basin, which 
then flows into South San Francisco Bay at the Flood Basin tide g ates. Matadero 
Creek flows vary with the season s; flows can be less than 0.03 MGD during 
summer. Below Discharge Point  002, Matadero Creek and Mayfield Slough are 
tidally -influenced  (the tide gates constrict, but do not preclude, tidal flows from 
entering and exiting the Flood Basin ).
Renzel Marsh Pond is an environmental enhancement project the Discharger 
initiated in 1992 to bring freshwater to the area to improve deteriorated habitat 
near the plant and to mitigate saltwater influx into Matadero Creek. The project 
created a 15 -acre fr eshwater pond from previously dry land. With a constructed 
berm around the pond to prevent stormwater inflows, treated effluent is the only 
flow into the pond. Because Renzel Marsh Pond was built in a former upland (dry) 
environment and is exclusively main tained by the plant’s discharge with a 
controlled outfall to Matadero Creek, the receiving water for Discharge Point  002 is 
Matadero Creek.
The Discharger plans to build a horizontal levee on the shore of Harbor Marsh in 
the Palo Alto Baylands . The horizontal levee w ould provide multiple  benefits , such 
as sea level rise adaptation, habitat enhancement, and additional wastewater 
treatment with  up to 0.1 MGD of plant effluent . The Discharger proposes to add 
the horizontal levee as a new outfall (proposed Discharge Point 003), as shown in

===PLANNED CHANGES===

Planned Changes
During the term of this Order, the Discharger plans to complete several plant 
improvements These include:
2.5.1 . An Advanced Water Purification System to improve recycled water quality using 
microfiltration followed by reverse osmosis to reduce the level of total dissolved 
solids.
2.5.2. Upgrades to the biological treatment process to enhance total inorganic 
nitrogen removal. The project w ould remove the fixed film reactors  from the 
treatment train and add nitrogen removal and membrane aerated biofilm reactor 
process units to the existing aeration basins to improve and intensify the 
treatment process.
2.5.3. A new parallel outfall line that w ould discharge next to the current outfall line at 
the same discharge location a s Discharge Point 001  (the unnamed channel) . 
This project would increase the treatment plant’s discharge capacity to 
compensate for head losses caused by sea level rise, high tides , and extreme 
wet-weather events. The increased discharge capacity would  remain below the 
current permitted flow capacity.
2.5.4. A Horizontal Levee Pilot Project would consist of construct ing a berm and 
horizontal levee along a portion of existing upland habitat adjacent to Harbor 
Marsh. The horizontal levee would be planted with various native plants to 
restore diverse transitional habitats , including freshwater marsh, wet meadow, 
and riparian scrub , and  would provide wave attenuation , sea level rise 
adaptation space , and refugia habitat for key species , such as the salt marsh 
harvest mouse. The project w ould include  install ing an underground pipeline to 
transport treated wastewater to the hor izontal levee for irrigation of the native 
plants. The Discharger plans to conduct monitoring and create community 
science opportunities to ensure that data will be available for use in the larger 
flood control levee projects for Palo Alto as well as regionally .
2.6. Sea Level Rise
The City of Palo Alto completed a Sea Level Rise Vulnerability Assessment  in 
2022  that identified several vulnerabilities related to  sea level rise and rising 
groundwater. The Discharger determined that its treatment plant is within the 
FEMA 100 -year flood plain and not currently protected by a FEMA -accredited 
levee.  The Discharger is partnering with the U.S. Army Corp of Engineers, Valley 
Water, and other regional stakeholders on a South San Francisco Bay Shoreline 
Phase II Feasibility Study  to address levee protection around Palo Alto. 
To limit flooding, t he Discharger has contingency procedures that include  
restricting sanitary sewer system flows and u sing surplus tanks for emergency 
storage capacity. Local flood control channels and pump stations have been
